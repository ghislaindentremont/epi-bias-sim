Why does the estimate not equal the truth?

An epidemiological estimate can miss the true exposure–disease relationship for three structural reasons: the wrong people were studied (selection), the right people were measured wrongly (measurement), or a third variable was left to mix its effect with the exposure's (confounding). Each module below builds a population where the truth is known, applies one mechanism, and lets you see the damage — and the repair — on a 2×2 table.

The shared toolkit

Every module uses the same conventions, so estimates are comparable across the three mechanisms.

  • Table layout. Exposed in the top row, diseased in the left column, so cell a is exposed-and-diseased. This matches epi.2by2(TAB, method = "cross.sectional") once the table has been rearranged with exposure first.
  • Effect measures. Prevalence odds ratio ad ⁄ bc, prevalence ratio [a⁄(a+b)] ⁄ [c⁄(c+d)], prevalence difference a⁄(a+b) − c⁄(c+d). Confidence intervals for ratios are computed on the log scale (Woolf), which is also what glm(family = "binomial") and confint() do.
  • The anchor population. NHANES 2009–12: 403 of 4,909 males and 357 of 4,949 females report diabetes, a prevalence OR of 1.15 for male sex. It is large enough to treat as a population and small enough that a biased sample's CI can exclude the truth.
  • Expected vs. random counts. By default each simulator applies the mechanism to expected counts, so what you see is the bias alone. Switch to a random draw to add sampling variability and to reproduce the R code exactly.
  • Colour. population or truth, what the analyst actually observes, what a correction recovers. A badge marks whether the observed 95% CI covers the truth.

How the modules build on each other

Selection and measurement act on a fixed 2×2 table: they redistribute or shrink cells, so the whole story is visible in the table arithmetic. Confounding needs a third variable, so its module generates individual-level data from a logistic model and analyses it the way you would in practice — stratify, pool with Mantel–Haenszel, add the covariate to glm, or re-weight with inverse probability of treatment weights. Each module ends with the R code that reproduces the current settings, so you can carry a scenario straight into your own script.

A note on truth. Because the population is simulated, the truth is known, and “bias” means the gap between the estimate and that truth in expectation — not the gap caused by chance. That distinction is why expected counts are the default: with a random draw, a CI that excludes the truth might simply be one of the 5%.
Module 01

Selection bias

Treat the NHANES sex–diabetes table as the population. Each of its four cells is sampled with its own probability (α, β, γ, δ). Selection that depends on exposure and disease jointly distorts the odds ratio by exactly αδ ⁄ βγ; inverse probability of selection weights undo it by dividing each sampled cell by its selection probability.

Module 02

Measurement bias

The same population, measured imperfectly. Self-reported diabetes misses undiagnosed cases (sensitivity below 1) and occasionally counts people who do not have it (specificity below 1); the same can happen to exposure. Non-differential misclassification of a binary variable usually pulls ratio measures toward the null; differential misclassification can push them anywhere.

Module 03

Confounding

A binary confounder Z (think “older and heavier”) shifts both the probability of exposure X (physical activity) and the risk of disease Y (diabetes). In NHANES the crude OR for physical activity is 0.45; adjusting for BMI, age and sex moves it to 0.82. Here the same thing happens with parameters you control, and every standard remedy is applied side by side.
